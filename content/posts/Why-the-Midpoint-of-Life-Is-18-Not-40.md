You have probably had that moment: scrolling through old photos, you suddenly realize that being 18 was more than a decade ago. The cicadas of your college-entrance-exam summer still seem to buzz in your ears, yet the calendar tells you plainly that you have traveled very, very far.

As children, summers stretched on without end — a single piece of candy, a breeze, could spend an entire afternoon. Now the four seasons feel fast-forwarded: the resolutions you made at New Year's barely get started before the year is already over.

We always say time moves faster as we grow older.

You might think this is just an illusion of growing up. Few people tell you the truth: it is not an illusion. If you measure by an objective lifespan of 80 years, the arithmetic midpoint of life is indeed 40. But if you measure by the "subjective life" you can actually feel and remember, the midpoint of your life was reached as early as 18.

Behind this counterintuitive conclusion lies the brain's most basic logic for perceiving the world — the law of logarithms.

## The Brain Never Uses Arithmetic; It Measures Time with Logarithms

There is a classic psychological law called the Weber–Fechner law. It reveals a truth: our subjective experience of all external stimuli never follows "absolute values" but "relative proportions."

Earning an extra 100 yuan feels like a windfall when your monthly salary is 3,000, yet feels utterly flat when it is 30,000. A single degree of brightness is blinding in the dark but almost unnoticed in sunlight. Our senses are naturally sensitive to "rate of change" and dull to "absolute magnitude."

Time is precisely the thing that fits this pattern best.

To a 5-year-old, one year is a fifth of an entire life — so long it seems endless.

To a 20-year-old, one year is a twentieth of life — merely one turn of the seasons.

To a 40-year-old, one year is a mere fortieth — gone in a blink, leaving no trace.

The older we get, the less weight each objectively equal stretch of time carries in our lives. The brain does not tally the years with addition and subtraction; it uses logarithms, it uses multiples. So our "subjective life" is never a straight line unfolding at constant speed, but a curve whose growth keeps accelerating.

## Why the Real Midpoint of Life Falls Exactly at 18

We can run a simple calculation.

People do not possess continuous autobiographical memory from birth. For most, clear and continuous life memories begin around age 4. So let us take 4 as the perceptual starting point and 80 as the end of life.

If we measure by linear arithmetic, the midpoint of life is:

> (4 + 80) ÷ 2 = 42 years old

This is half of objective time — what we commonly call "middle age."

But if we measure by logarithmic perception, the midpoint of subjective experience is not the arithmetic mean. The Weber–Fechner law states that subjective perceptual intensity *S* is proportional to the natural logarithm of *I*, where *I* represents age. We look for the midpoint of subjective memory, where the total subjective perception before and after it is equal:

> ln(x) − ln(4) = ln(80) − ln(x)
> ln(x/4) = ln(80/x)
> x ÷ 4 = 80 ÷ x
> x × x = 4 × 80
> x = √(4 × 80) ≈ 18

What does this number mean?

It means the 14 years from age 4 to 18 deliver roughly the same total subjective experience as the full 62 years from 18 to 80.

All the firsts and palpitations, the confusion and recklessness, the joys and tears of your childhood and youth — those tiny yet vivid days, those profound yet burning moments — their combined weight in your life's memories is almost equal to your entire adulthood of several decades.

This is why we always feel that the fullest, longest stretch of life stays frozen at 18. It is not that later days grew shorter; it is that each later year grew lighter in your perception.

## Why Time Begins to Accelerate After 18

Eighteen is an invisible watershed, separating two entirely different rhythms of life.

In our youth, life is an expedition ever expanding outward. The first day of school, the first time away from home, the first flutter of the heart, the first failure, the first glimpse of a distant landscape… the world unfolds before us layer by layer, and every day pours new cognition into the brain. These countless "firsts" generate a wealth of independent memory fragments, filling the years full and long. Looking back, every step is clearly distinguishable, so those days felt slow and life felt long.

But after adulthood, life slowly settles into fixed tracks.

Commuting, working, off-duty, resting — similar workdays repeat, familiar scenes return day after day. The brain is by nature a lazy organ; it will not record highly similar experiences over and over, but packs and compresses them into a vague impression. So years pass, and looking back it feels like only a day has gone by. Time "fast-forwards" silently.

The more essential difference is this: before 18, we are desperately building the self and learning the rules of the world, with fresh growth every year; after 18, our worldview gradually sets, and we mostly iterate repeatedly within an existing framework. Curiosity about the world fades, and our perception of life slowly numbs.

When new experiences grow fewer, the "thickness" of life stops growing. The truth behind time's acceleration was never that time shortened, but that our memories grew sparse.

## Only by Understanding the Logarithmic Law Do We Understand What a Life Worth Living Is

Many who grasp this pattern fall into a vague anxiety: so the most vivid part of life is long past, and the remaining days are merely an acceleration toward the end.

Yet I believe the most precious part of this model is precisely that it tells us: the texture of time has always been in our own hands.

It is never fatalism — it does not say life loses its value after 18. It is a reminder: the value of life never lies in its length, but in its density; not in how many days you lived, but in how many days you truly remember.

If you want to live this life well, the answer is hidden in the logic of time itself.

## First, Break Repetition on Purpose, and "Encrypt" Your Memories

Since repeated experiences get compressed by the brain, let us deliberately introduce variables into life.

You need not resign and travel far, overturning everything. Even changing your route home, learning a skill you have never touched, trying a dish you have never made, or walking through an unfamiliar city — these fresh stimuli restart the brain's recording mode and "stretch" the compressed years once more.

What truly ages people is never age itself, but the loss of freshness toward life and the cessation of exploring the world. As long as you keep embracing new things, your subjective time is still growing.

## Second, Use "Peak Experiences" to Anchor the Weight of Life

We do not need every day to be heart-stopping, but we should create some "memory anchors" for ourselves.

It is the sense of achievement from throwing yourself wholly into finishing something; the tender moments spent with someone who matters; the instant you break past your own boundary; those moments when you genuinely feel "alive." Like lighthouses, these moments will, when you look back on life, hold that stretch of years full and solid.

Ordinary days scatter with the wind, but those burning, sincere, invested moments stay in your life forever, becoming the weight of your existence.

## Third, Always Keep a Sense of "Strangeness" Toward the World

Time moved slowly in childhood essentially because everything was strange. We looked at the world with all our curiosity; a single falling leaf or an ant could be studied for half a day.

The most terrifying thing in adulthood is to grow numb and take things for granted — to feel there is nothing new under the sun, to be blind to the beauty around us, to disdain the unknown. When the world shrinks, time naturally speeds up.

True maturity is never about becoming worldly and slick, but about still being able, after seeing through the truth of life, to be curious about a flower like a child, to be moved by a breeze, to hold expectations for the unknown. Keeping this "strangeness" is keeping the essence of youth.

## Fourth, Measure Life by Depth, Not Length

Some live to eighty, yet looking back see only a blur, thin as a blank sheet of paper;

Others live only a few short decades, yet experience countless burning moments, their lives as thick as a book.

Whether a life is worth it never depends on how many years you lived, but on whether you truly lived. Is it walking through a script others set, or experiencing, creating, loving, and erring by your own will, living into a unique self?

## Finally

Eighteen is beautiful — it is the most vivid age of our lives, full of infinite possibility.

But eighteen is never the peak of life, still less its end.

We cannot escape time's acceleration, but we can choose to add weight to time.

Go experience, go feel, go love, go break.

Let every year hold new scenery, every age new growth.

So that when you are old and look back on life, you will not feel only that time flew by, an empty dream.

You will clearly remember those moments of weeping and laughing, those days and nights you gave your all, those times of loving and being loved.

Strung together, they are your one-of-a-kind, worth-living life.

After all, life was never meant for rushing through; it was meant for feeling.
